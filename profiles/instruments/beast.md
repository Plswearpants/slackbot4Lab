# beast

- kind: instrument

- source: https://lair.phas.ubc.ca/instruments/high-magnetic-field-ultra-low-temperature-stm-beast/
- updated: 2026-08-19

## Abstract

The Beast is a homebuilt scanning tunnelling microscope designed in the late 2000s and constructed in the early 2010s, combining high magnetic field with ultra-low temperature operation. A dilution refrigerator reaches temperatures at the STM head of about 100 mK. A vector magnet allows up to 7 T in the z-direction and 2 T into the x-y plane of the sample, so the sample's magnetic moment, spin orientation and magnetic anisotropy can be measured with high spatial resolution.

It sits on an 80-metric-ton concrete block in one of the quietest experimental rooms in North America. Research focuses on superconductors, magnetic materials, 2D materials and nanoscale devices.

## Systems

FeSe, PER, LaSbTe, AuSn4, IVC, WFM, EDI, JEOL, PHAS, CRWR, HEO, NEG

### 2026

Over the winter and into spring, the Beast was largely occupied with a stubborn LaSbTe sample and a chronic mechanical noise problem. James worked through repeated benchtop cleaves and post remounts (H20E epoxy, later switching to TorrSeal after a post came off but old epoxy stayed behind), discovering at one point that the crystal's center was riddled with silver epoxy, and repeatedly re-seated the sample plate after "impact events" that periodically demolished whatever clean, scannable area had just been found — one such impact striking right after a stable tip and good herringbone/stripe imaging had finally been achieved. Recurring impedance tests, LN2/LHe transfers, and auto-approaches on Au(111) were used to keep the system characterized between LaSbTe attempts, and Vanessa did some remote-control testing on the Beast's Nanonis computer, restoring the cable and software afterward (noting a version mismatch with her RC software). James spent considerable effort chasing the impact-noise source, eventually jury-rigging the helium exhaust/recovery line (the "balloon," swapped for a bigger tube, then properly plumbed into recovery) and reasoning through it via LRC/damping/Q-factor analogies, with a 4-hour impact-free stretch reported by Feb 24. In early February the DR fridge was put into dilution mode (manual Lakeshore entries used due to a missing VI), reaching ~180 mK at the mixing chamber and ~500 mK at the sample plate over a slow, multi-day cooldown; auto-approach and scanning at base temperature showed only minor transient heating on landing, though surface quality remained poor. A NEG replacement quote was obtained from SAES (referencing the $9061 paid for the Z500 in 2024, with discount/warranty terms discussed with Markus), and Beast compressor maintenance was flagged as pending amid the broader lab reorganization for MBE decommissioning and OMBE/microwave work in room 145.

### 2025

The Beast's 2025 was dominated by a series of hardware crises and recoveries centered on the sample-exchange jaws and cryostat: the year opened with James troubleshooting a Nanonis RC4 controller left dead by a Christmas power event, replacing the helium exhaust balloon, and building new mounting plates for thick HEO crystals, followed by a successful cleave/load/scan campaign on a 5RE HEO in late January that produced clean topography (and an apparent isostructural NCCO result) despite some multi-tipped, "messy" early images requiring field-emission tip treatment; on Feb 5 the exhaust balloon burst and, separately, the jaws dropped a sample plate back into the STM on the way to the main chamber, triggering an eight-attempt, all-failed "fishing" recovery effort before James pulled the IVC and radiation shield, retrieved the plate from the cleaver basket, reinstalled the cleaving arm, etched and reinstalled the tip, adjusted the tension plate, and closed up for a pump-down that passed all tests, leading into a full bake (Feb 19–24) with NEG conditioning and activation; post-bake, coarse motion was confirmed working at 77 K (having failed at room temperature), a shark valve was installed, and LN2/LHe transfers resumed, but a March 7 LHe fill left the magnet leads frozen in place, cutting hold time from 7 to 4-5 days—James's manual freeing attempts and a dewar warmer failed, and it was Jisun who, working the top retaining screw roughly 1/8 turn per day starting March 25, finally freed the lead on March 26 (still cold to the touch); after James's travel, the team resumed with humidity-strip moisture checks before an April 2 LN2 cooldown, incremental LN2 top-ups, a clean LHe transfer on April 10 (magnet leads now cooperating), and ongoing Au(111) sputter/anneal cycles, impact-noise investigations, and a recovery-line modification through April; a second jaws failure dropped a sample plate again on May 1 during main-chamber entry, prompting extensive jaws testing across all three sample plates and development of a new sample-exchange protocol by mid-May, followed by another wrap-and-bake cycle, post-bake impedance and walker testing, LN2 precooling, and by early June Jisun had topped up LN2, re-approached, and begun tip treatment (with a Tesla tip) on a newly found clean area for James to continue.

### 2024

In 2024 the Beast STM system, tended chiefly by James with Jisun, Sarah, Doug, and Dong Chen contributing diagnostics and sample science, cycled through cryogen logistics, tip and sample failures, and a persistent noise hunt. January began with LN2 top-ups, an impedance sanity check, control-computer updates/restarts, and LHe transfers on 17–18 January to bring the system cold again after the purifier plugged (reported by Bobby), delaying subsequent fills into late January; Au(111) was resputtered and annealed and loaded into the STM. Through late January and February the team removed coin gold, ran impedance tests at 4 K, tested walker motion, and rediscovered "impact noise" traced tentatively to either the LHe bath/recovery system or a Bi-2212-contaminated tip; extensive tip work followed, including field emission and aggressive shaping on gold and coin-gold (sputtered at 1.5 keV for 30 min on 7 Feb) to isolate the noise source, culminating in removal of the shared helium flow-meter from the recovery line on 14 Feb to test its contribution. AuSn4 mounting proved troublesome: cleave attempts on 20–22 February failed when the post popped off the crystal and later took the whole crystal with it, exposing flux-agglomerate issues in the boule and prompting consultation with Alannah's crystal-growth group; a successful RT cleave was finally achieved 28–29 February, only for the subsequent approach to fail because Torr Seal alone (rather than H20E) had been used between the Ta plate and copper block, creating an insulating short that also crashed and likely damaged the tip, forcing re-preparation of both Au(111) and AuSn4. Hardware maintenance included a compressor oil change and LN2 trap top-up (26 Feb), while on 6 March the dilution fridge developed a partial impedance plug attributed to a missed trap fill in mid-February, prompting a new protocol of topping the trap at every LHe transfer and consideration of warming the insert to 300 K; a second ping-pong valve was slated for installation to address ongoing noise. Jisun's detailed noise-spectrum monitoring (7–13 March) showed dewar-driven noise events independent of external disturbances, correlated with sample-plate seating and post-cooldown relaxation, spurring discussion of a soft rubber buffer bag before the first ping-pong ball valve. The STM jaws popped a second time on 13–14 March during AuSn4 reseating but were recovered without breaking vacuum. By mid-March the team successfully located and imaged an AuSn4 surface showing a flat plane with slight tilt and a surface reconstruction closely resembling that reported in the Nature Communications p-wave superconductivity paper, setting up bias-dependent STS work to probe possible CDW or tip-state origins of the observed gap.

### 2023

The Beast spent 2023 fighting a persistent intermittent "impact noise" and a jammed z-arm, with James Day as the primary hands-on operator supported by Sarah and Jisun on cryogenics and diagnostics. The year opened with a warm-up, thermometry cable checks, and a full LN2 trap teardown after a leak traced to a bad KF centering ring, followed by post-clean impedance tests at room T and 4K; the prep chamber was vented to fix a loose wire, then wrapped and baked before Au(111) re-prep and scanning resumed. LHe logistics were a running theme, shared across Beast, Tesla, and Folk-group demands, with Jisun tracking recovery rates via the gas meter (e.g., 76.8 L in for a 64% fill, later fills of 68.8 L and 69.4 L collected during warm-ups) and diagnosing backpressure needs (50–100+ psi) for a dewar suspected of a leak. Noise troubleshooting cycled between the macor sample plate — where James found and filed down oversized screws causing sapphire bearings to rattle, without resolving the noise — and a switch to an all-metal sample plate with coin gold, which stopped producing impact events and became the interim working configuration while Bi-2212 was prepped for cold-cleave and loading. From mid-March, the z-arm's jaws and ~2m control rod seized, prompting venting/opening of the main chamber, removal of the control kit (which didn't fix it), and an extended repair correspondence with Marc at Ferrovac, weighing options from on-site refurbishment to shipping the manipulator insert back to Switzerland or building a replacement. By April, James and Doug Wong were designing and machining a custom lifting hoist to safely pull the z-arm insert, resolving SolidWorks/CADPass issues to consult Ferrovac's 3D model, baking and pumping the GHS trap (catching a minor plug before warm-up), and disassembling/cleaning the sample boat and macor plate in parallel. By early May the lifting jig was installed in preparation for z-arm removal, though a small part needed re-machining before the multi-person lift (~5 people) could proceed.

### 2022

The Beast spent the first quarter of 2022 in a slow, methodical rebuild before falling into a stubborn electromechanical mystery that would dominate James, Jisun, and Sarah's year: January began with James removing, modifying, and re-installing heat-sinking braids, cooling the GHS LN2 trap, restarting mixture circulation to clean it, and installing the IVC, while he and Jisun spent weeks hand-tuning the tension plate (initially far too tight) and doing cable surgery on port D, finding and band-aid-fixing a broken pin connection on the CSI (inner capacitive sensor) line so the capacitance-encoder (CE) readings finally became sensible; by early February a copper gasket sealed the IVC, the system was pumped down, wrapped, and baked (17 Feb–25 Feb), with the NEG in the main chamber reactivated afterward since it had sat exposed to air for weeks. Testing resumed with auto-approach on Au(111), and repeatedly showed a "mechanical-but-not-electrical" crash — the Nanonis capacitive encoder and noise spectrum analyzer indicating contact while the tunneling line showed no continuity from bias — a problem that persisted whether using the macor sample plate or a coin-gold-on-all-metal plate, and survived a full disassembly/cleaning/reassembly of the macor plate, field-emission attempts to blow debris off the tip, and even a tip re-cut by Sarah and Jisun in room 145 in mid-March after discovering a split tip end. A second IVC bake (17–21 March) and continued troubleshooting through April failed to resolve it; systematic resistance measurements comparing the Beast's Nanonis controller to the lab's Createc and Omicron systems (by Jisun and James) revealed anomalously high and unstable shield-to-ground resistances (115 Ω rising to ~160 Ω with bias changes, versus 1–4 Ω on the other systems, with AO8 behaving differently from other analog outputs), pointing to a possible grounding configuration issue tied to internal dip-switches whose prior configuration Sarah could not confirm; further tests showed the "crashed" tip could "uncrash" when bias was dropped from 1 V to 1–10 mV (giving ~700 pA–6 nA, suggestive of a megohm-scale contact), and disconnecting the preamp casing ground induced large but sub-saturating noise while still triggering the preamp overload light — unexplained behavior that remained open. By mid-April James shimmed the Au(111) macor sample plate upward by 0.37 mm and checked SolidWorks for mechanical interference with prism motion, but by 21 April the sprint goal was simply logged as "endless troubleshooting," with the grounding/shorting mystery still unresolved at year's first-quarter close.

### 2021

James led the Beast through a long push to get atomic resolution and a working cooldown over 2021: after weeks of tip prep and re-approaches in late January, he got the first clean atomic images on Au(111) on Jan 27–28 (with Jisun helping troubleshoot the double/triple-tip artifacts), and the team then began cooling the DR to base temperature. A capacitive-sensor (CE) fault in early February caused a jaws/Z-arm mishap during a LHe transfer — with the preamp overload light flagging a bad retract — prompting Sarah and Jisun to dig into the coarse-approach VI waveforms and question the CE's reliability, eventually considering bypassing it for step-counted approaches or a capacitance-bridge/lock-in alternative. James continued tip conditioning through field emission pulses (150 V) and, by Feb 5–6, was getting clean 300 nm × 300 nm scans of Au(111) showing surface state, step edges, and multiple screw dislocations (spotted by Sarah). Aluminum work followed: on Feb 10, James ran sputter/anneal cycles (1–1.5 keV, 500 °C, 30–40 min) and cooled into the STM without incident, though the still hung at 1.1 K, the mixing chamber reached 130 mK, the sample plate 510 mK, and the copper ring 920 mK — pointing to a still problem and an added heat load from the sample plate. James also noted the DR's He3/He4 isotopic ratio was low (18% vs. the recommended ~25%) and planned adding 6.7 L of 33% mixture. Separately, the Ferrovac in-vacuum manipulator's magnetic couplings (transmitter and high-torque) had been dislocating and losing rotational coupling, and after venting and consulting Ferrovac, James reported by March 3 that the jaws appeared fixed. In parallel, Ashley began scoping a fiber-optic bundle/lens system for in-situ optical imaging (settling on a ~4–5 mm field of view at 20-micron resolution, per discussion with Doug, James, and Sarah), while confirming silicone resin is not UHV-safe. Finally, in mid-March the team restarted a full cooldown: Jisun transferred LN2 on March 16, James sputter/annealed Au(111) once more and loaded it into the STM on March 18 (after fixing a new LabVIEW z-arm VI bug traced to a loose cable, resolved by disconnect/reconnect and a computer restart), and — with Ashley's help — began the LHe transfer on March 19 to bring the Beast back to base temperature for further work.

### 2020

James spent much of January and February wrestling the Beast back into shape and onto FeSe: a mysterious ~0.7 Hz dripping/noise source traced to the LN2 during cooldown (spikes around 220 Hz and 400 Hz persisted briefly after approach) was resolved by fully boiling off and then redoing a proper LN2 fill on 1/20, after which the herringbone reconstruction on Au(111) reappeared and the background noise vanished; James also completed the LHe recovery line for the Beast pod that same night, and dewar #1465 was readied for use on 1/23. Through late January and early February, James, Jisun, and others cycled between Au(111) imaging/spectroscopy (bias sweeps at 77 K and 4 K, tunneling currents up to 400 pA, scan areas up to 200 nm², with recurring tip instability above 2.5–3 V dropping material onto the surface) and repeated field-emission tip treatments — including a 20-minute FE at 150 V/10 µA (sample positive) on 2/4 logged in the pod notebook, with polarity flipped and continued the next morning. New cabling was installed by James for the FE setup on 2/4, and an HV power supply was shared between the Beast and antechamber setups by Seokhwan. By 2/6 the Au(111) surface had degraded, prompting removal of the gold sample for re-prep and loading of FeSe; the cleave went badly, leaving a flake hanging off the crystal and damaging the sample-plate post, so James instead ran two rounds of sputter-anneal on a fresh Au(111) plate (keeping orientation fixed) and reloaded it into the STM that night. The system then spent several days slowly cooling — pumps spun down 2/7, auto-approach attempted but incomplete due to residual warmth in the sample plate — with Jisun and James trading off overnight monitoring and remote logins into early February, aiming to resume FeSe prep once Au re-cooling and re-characterization were complete.

### 2019

The Beast spent early 2019 sidelined awaiting parts and a replacement dry pump, with James practicing mounting and cleaving FeSe samples on 2mm-bore ceramic rods while machined sample-plate components (dropped off by Harish) and pump paperwork dragged on; the new dry pump finally arrived and was plumbed in by February 21, and the whole system was powered down and unplugged March 1 ahead of the March 9 building shutdown. After the March Meeting hiatus the system came back to life May 13, and following heavy remote-desktop use by James, Jisun ran a full walker test on May 26 (confirmed working) and kicked off an auto-approach that landed around 61590, after which piezo calibration was set to "250 mK (tentative)" with new RT/LN2 calibration values added to the control software and overnight 100nm² scans begun; an impedance leak check on May 28 gave 0.009 mbar/min (versus 0.0119–0.0125 mbar/min in 2017), judged acceptable without a bake. LHe cooldown began May 28–30 using dewar 2428 (filled to 104L, later 80.3%), with a level-meter sensor cable found unplugged near the KF flange/siphon port by Doug, an accidental open He gas cylinder (1380 units remaining) closed by Seokhwan, and subsequent smaller top-up fills (~50L) plus a newly ordered gas cylinder. Through early-to-mid June the fridge suffered a mysterious problem where T_still and T_stm hung around 13–20K for hours during cooldown, auto-approaches stalled or produced anomalous noise spectra (mountain-shaped turning to foam-like), and dI/dV/I-V readings intermittently failed to go negative; the team diagnosed this as a sample-plate seating/bias-contact issue tied to physical contact with the tip, tried a manipulator-arm reseating trick, eventually landed and tunneled (with hot temperature readings) on June 4, and stabilized scanning near 15K by June 5, though the tip repeatedly dumped debris and required pulsing and later field-emission tip conditioning (2–3 shots, high to low power) planned by Jisun. By June 13 a flaky CE reader and a bias-contact loss after an accidental tip-sample crash triggered several failed re-approaches (0/3) on the Au sample, prompting talk of superstition-driven procedure repeats, contingency plans to cleave a fresh FeSe sample under cold N2 flow, and a backup HOPG mount, with James finally succeeding on attempt 4 the night of June 13 before Jisun departed for five weeks of travel.

### 2018

The Beast spent much of early 2018 fighting a mysterious pressure and walker-motion crisis: on January 2nd Aaron Kraft found the central chamber at 3x10^-10 mbar (cold cathode) with the getter gauge reading 1.1x10^-7, and after cycling turbos and gate valves the insert pressure climbed an order of magnitude to 9.8x10^-7 before settling; RGA scans (run by Aaron and Jisun) turned up a stubborn, dominant mass-40 peak that the team spent days puzzling over (Katherine weighing in as resident chemist), eventually suspecting decomposed keyboard-duster propellant or some contact/outgassing artifact rather than a true leak, since N2 and mass-40 baselines taken by isolating each chamber (insert, central chamber, sample storage) behaved like a well-baked system. After a first LHe transfer on January 12–13 (two small dewars, James bringing helium gas, magnet warming and re-cooling), the STM walker got stuck repeatedly on the coarse-motion counter (CE) around 21000–22000, requiring James and Jisun to experiment with steps-per-pulse settings (1 to 512) and pulse voltage before freeing it at 4 steps/pulse; removing the sample plate let the walker reach CE=20000 but it then refused to retract, prompting suspicion of a tension/contact problem rather than a crash. The team warmed the insert deliberately with small helium gas shots into the vacuum can (James, with a reminder not to run piezos during warming), dropped the dewar on January 23 (James, Jisun), and Jisun ran walker and leak tests through late January, finding CE barely moved. A timed activation of the getter ran long to 540°C on January 30 before Aaron and James caught it and ended it manually; Jisun then wrapped the insert in heating tape (finding one broken/open-circuit tape segment, set aside and labeled), reseated a dislodged thermocouple, and added blankets ahead of a full bake, which Graham shut down on February 10 with P_STM at 7.0x10^-8 mbar, P_CENT at 4.0x10^-8 mbar, and the dewar jacket at 2.41x10^-5 mbar. By February 15 the walker was confirmed working again, and on February 18 James set up a new auto-approach with a freshly cooled sample plate, though the approach curve showed erratic hiccups (a notable stall at CE=24475, false landings, and 60 Hz noise with tunneling current pinned at 50 pA) that left the instrument still not fully approached and scanning by month's end.

### 2017

The Beast spent 2017 cycling repeatedly through bake-outs, cooldowns, tip crashes, and re-approaches under Brandon Stuart, Graham (GAB), and James's supervision: in January the chamber was pumped and approached (Jan 20-21) for a gold sample continuous scan at 250nm x 250nm with tunable bias, followed by routine LN2 transfers and dewar raising/dropping through February (Feb 7, 16, 18) after the sample prism stopped retracting, forcing a warm-up. In March, Python/temperature-logger issues recurred (Mar 8) requiring Graham's troubleshooting, and the team began archiving old spectra to benchmark 20Hz noise floors against current performance. April saw a full reload cycle: a new sample hot-sputtered at 475°C for 10 minutes (1.2uA sputter current above background, Apr 9), followed by approach via LabVIEW/Nanonis (steps-per-pulse=1), a helium transfer (Apr 11), and diagnosis of a piezo-stack/pulse-generator fault traced with an oscilloscope per Rob's instructions (Apr 14, unresolved). LabVIEW itself expired its evaluation license (Apr 21), requiring reversion to an older ("Biebs") version and coordination with Hongyun; a subsequent COM-port mixup (COM3 vs COM1 vs COM4) blocked z-arm motor control until Brandon identified COM4 as correct (Apr 25). The tip/sample crashed on Apr 22 (current pinned at 1.057nA against a 100pA setpoint), requiring coarse step-backs and re-approach, achieved after 569 steps on Apr 22. By May, the load-lock turbo was restarted to retrieve the gold sample for inspection, and Graham monitored bake-out thermometry (T1 steady at 111°C, T2/T4 rising to ~77/67°C, T3 near the small z-arm fluctuating and stabilized near 112°C via variac adjustments) with chamber pressure logged at 6.5e-6 Torr, James cautioning to keep temperatures under ~110°C.
